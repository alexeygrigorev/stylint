# The AI Engineer Job Market

The title "AI Engineer" is still relatively new, so we wanted to understand it through market demand rather than intuition. We analyzed more than 1,000 job descriptions from a large technology job site. The locations were Berlin, Amsterdam, London, Los Angeles and New York. The sample showed how companies actually use the title, while also making clear that the percentages describe this dataset rather than a permanent definition.

## Classifying the market

An LLM classified 889 descriptions, of which about 70 percent were AI-first roles working directly on LLM and GenAI systems. Their responsibilities included RAG and agents. They also covered evaluation and production deployment, close to the usual picture of an AI Engineer building product features.

About 28.5 percent were AI-support roles. These focused on internal AI platforms, GPU and inference infrastructure. They also covered data pipelines, deployment and monitoring tools, or interfaces for prompts and experiments.

Fewer than 2 percent were traditional ML or deep learning roles. Their work looked more like ML Engineer or Research Engineer work, even though the job title said AI Engineer.

The largest category suggested a practical definition: an AI Engineer owns the design, evaluation and production operation of systems built on foundation models. The roles were 95.6 percent production-focused. Nearly 50 percent were backend-heavy, and about 20 percent were often full-stack.

## The skills behind the title

The most distinctive cluster was GenAI work. RAG appeared in 35.9 percent of descriptions, prompt engineering in 29.1 percent and LLM integration in 25.4 percent. Agents appeared in 14.4 percent, while fine-tuning appeared in 8.5 percent. The result suggested that employers valued architecting and operating GenAI systems more than prompt writing or pure model training in isolation.

Python appeared in 82.5 percent of roles. TypeScript appeared in 23.4 percent, React in 14.8 percent and FastAPI in 10.7 percent. Those numbers point toward application builders who need web APIs and sometimes user interfaces around AI systems.

Operations and cloud were also central. Operations appeared in 17.4 percent of descriptions and cloud in 13.4 percent. AWS appeared in 40.1 percent, Docker in 31.0 percent, CI/CD in 29.3 percent and Kubernetes in 29.1 percent. Azure appeared in 23.9 percent and GCP in 23.0 percent, so familiarity with more than one cloud can be useful.

Classical ML appeared less often, at 9.5 percent of mentions overall. Within that group, PyTorch appeared in 22.0 percent of roles and TensorFlow in 12.9 percent. Model training appeared in 6.4 percent and evaluation in 4.5 percent. Databases were 6.2 percent overall, with vector databases at 10.8 percent and PostgreSQL at 9.3 percent.

## Frameworks are not the definition

The framework results reinforced a distinction between architecture and brand names. LangChain appeared in 18.8 percent of roles, LangGraph in 8.0 percent and LlamaIndex in 5.8 percent. No single GenAI framework dominated. Infrastructure tools were more standardized and production-critical than any one application framework.

The day-to-day work grouped into three layers. The first covered end-to-end LLM applications, and the second covered retrieval and internal platforms. Secondary work included interfaces, performance, fine-tuning or security. For this market sample, the durable skill was turning foundation models into dependable, observable product features that run in production.
