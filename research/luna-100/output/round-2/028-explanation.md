# Reading the AI Engineer Market Through Job Descriptions

The title "AI Engineer" is new enough that a job description is often more useful than a fixed definition. In our study, we analyzed more than 1,000 descriptions across five cities. They were Berlin, Amsterdam, London, Los Angeles and New York. An LLM classified 889 of them into three categories. The percentages describe this dataset, so they're evidence about the market rather than guarantees about every role.

## Three kinds of work

About 70 percent were AI-first roles, working directly on LLM and GenAI systems. Their responsibilities included RAG, agents, evaluation and production deployment. They were closest to the familiar picture of an engineer building AI product features.

About 28.5 percent were AI-support roles whose work surrounded the models. It included internal AI platforms, GPU and inference infrastructure. It also covered data pipelines, deployment and monitoring tools, plus interfaces for prompts and experimentation.

Fewer than 2 percent were traditional ML or deep learning roles. Those descriptions mentioned scikit-learn, XGBoost and PyTorch. They also mentioned TensorFlow, computer vision and recommendations. The work looked more like ML Engineer or Research Engineer work.

From the largest category, we drew a practical definition. An engineer owns the design, evaluation and production operation of systems built on foundation models. The roles were 95.6 percent production-focused. Nearly 50 percent were backend-heavy, and about 20 percent were often full-stack.

## Follow the skill frequencies

RAG was the strongest distinctive GenAI indicator, appearing in 35.9 percent of descriptions. Prompt engineering appeared in 29.1 percent and LLM integration in 25.4 percent. Agents appeared in 14.4 percent, while fine-tuning appeared in 8.5 percent. The ordering suggests that employers value architecting and operating GenAI systems more than prompt writing or model training in isolation.

Python appeared in 82.5 percent of roles, while TypeScript appeared in 23.4 percent. React appeared in 14.8 percent and FastAPI in 10.7 percent. An engineer preparing for this market therefore needs Python, web APIs and at least some modern frontend familiarity.

Operations appeared in 17.4 percent of descriptions and cloud in 13.4 percent. AWS appeared in 40.1 percent, Docker in 31.0 percent, CI/CD in 29.3 percent and Kubernetes in 29.1 percent. Azure appeared in 23.9 percent and GCP in 23.0 percent. These tools form a common infrastructure baseline for deployment and automation.

Traditional ML remained useful context rather than the center of the role. Classical ML accounted for 9.5 percent of mentions overall. Within that group, PyTorch appeared in 22.0 percent of roles and TensorFlow in 12.9 percent. Databases accounted for 6.2 percent, with vector databases at 10.8 percent and PostgreSQL at 9.3 percent.

## Learn architectures before frameworks

Framework mentions were spread across several tools, with LangChain appearing at 18.8 percent, while LangGraph appeared at 8.0 percent and LlamaIndex at 5.8 percent. No single framework dominated, making architectural understanding more durable than loyalty to a particular library.

We grouped the work into layers. Core responsibilities include end-to-end LLM applications such as RAG and agents. They also include APIs, deployment, monitoring, evaluation and guardrails. Common work includes proprietary-data retrieval, pipelines, internal platforms, agent workflows and product collaboration. Some roles add frontend interfaces or performance work.

Others add fine-tuning, self-hosting, customer implementations or security.

The study points toward a practical preparation strategy. Learn how to turn foundation models into dependable, observable product features. Then learn the cloud, container and infrastructure tools needed to run them. The exact titles and libraries may change, while these system responsibilities remain visible in the dataset.
