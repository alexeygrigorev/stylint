# What 1,000 AI Engineer Job Descriptions Showed Us

The title “AI Engineer” is relatively new, and conversations about it often mix several different jobs. We wanted to understand how companies used the title in practice, so we analyzed more than 1,000 job descriptions from a large technology job site. The sample covered Berlin, Amsterdam, London, Los Angeles, and New York.

The first step was classification. We asked an LLM to put 889 descriptions into three broad groups. About 70 percent were AI-first roles. These jobs worked directly on LLM and generative AI systems, including RAG, agents, evaluation, and production deployment. Roughly 28.5 percent were AI-support roles focused on the surrounding infrastructure: internal AI platforms, GPU and inference systems, data pipelines, deployment tooling, monitoring, or interfaces for prompts and experiments. Fewer than 2 percent were traditional ML or deep learning roles carrying the AI Engineer title.

That distribution suggested a practical definition: an AI Engineer owns the design, evaluation, and production operation of systems built on foundation models. The roles were overwhelmingly production-focused, at 95.6 percent, and nearly half were backend-heavy. About 20 percent were often full-stack. The picture was closer to a Python-based, cloud-native application builder than to a researcher training models from scratch.

The skill mentions reinforced that view. RAG appeared in 35.9 percent of descriptions, making it the strongest distinctive generative-AI signal. Prompt engineering appeared in 29.1 percent, LLM integration in 25.4 percent, agents in 14.4 percent, and fine-tuning in 8.5 percent. The market seemed to care more about architecting and operating a generative-AI system than about prompt writing or model training as isolated skills.

Python was mentioned in 82.5 percent of the roles. TypeScript appeared in 23.4 percent, React in 14.8 percent, and FastAPI in 10.7 percent, which suggested that many engineers were expected to build APIs and sometimes interfaces around the AI system. Operations and cloud appeared in 17.4 and 13.4 percent of descriptions, respectively.

Infrastructure tools were more standardized than AI frameworks. AWS appeared in 40.1 percent of postings, Docker in 31 percent, CI/CD in 29.3 percent, and Kubernetes in 29.1 percent. Azure and GCP appeared in 23.9 and 23 percent. By contrast, LangChain appeared in 18.8 percent, LangGraph in 8 percent, and LlamaIndex in 5.8 percent. No framework dominated, which made architectural understanding more durable than loyalty to a particular library.

The day-to-day work fell into layers. Core responsibilities involved end-to-end LLM applications such as RAG systems and agents, with APIs, deployment, monitoring, evaluation, and guardrails. Common responsibilities included proprietary-data retrieval, pipelines, internal platforms, agent workflows, and collaboration with product teams. Some roles also included frontend work, optimization, fine-tuning, self-hosting, customer implementations, security, or compliance.

The exact tools and titles will keep changing. The common thread in the data was already visible: turning foundation models into dependable product features that run in production.
