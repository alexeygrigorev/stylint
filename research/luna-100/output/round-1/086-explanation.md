# A Repeatable AI Engineering Job Search

The order is flexible in practice. A conversation can change the project you build, and an interview can reveal a missing skill to study. The value is in continuing to connect the activities and recording what each round teaches you.

The job search becomes easier to manage when it is treated as a loop rather than a single application. Network to learn the market, apply to relevant roles, build projects that show the needed skills, interview, record what happened, improve, and repeat.

Networking gives context that job boards do not. Speak with people working as AI engineers, ask about their projects and tools, and learn whether their companies hire. Junior engineers who recently found jobs can explain which preparation helped and what they would change. A conversation can be useful even when it does not produce a referral.

Apply before every requirement feels satisfied. Job descriptions describe an ideal candidate, so do not interpret every bullet as a mandatory gate. There are sensible limits: a junior should usually avoid a senior role, and matching only a small portion of the requirements is a warning. For relevant roles, however, let the hiring team evaluate you. If applications produce no response, inspect the CV and make its evidence relevant to those roles.

Use the market research to choose small projects. Note recurring technologies and timebox each project to 10–20 hours while exploring. A portfolio project should have public code, a clear README, and a working result. Share the idea, milestones, screenshots, and final link so other people can see how you work. The deeper work can come after you understand which direction fits.

Prepare for several interview formats. Recruiters ask about the company, motivation, availability, salary, and your background. Technical rounds may cover LLMs, RAG, agents, evaluation, monitoring, or coding. A project deep dive tests whether your CV matches reality. System design asks you to clarify requirements, data, and scale before sketching components. Behavioral interviews require examples of collaboration and setbacks.

Take-home tasks need boundaries. Ask how the result is evaluated, whether AI tools are allowed, and what the expected effort is. Build tests, an evaluation set, a README, and a demo before submission. A task that claims two hours but appears to require several days is a warning sign, as is an automated assignment sent before any conversation. It can be reasonable to decline when the time cost is too high and point to comparable public work.

After every interview, keep a journal. Record questions, difficult parts, and better answers for next time. Rejection may reflect a changed role, an internal hire, or a freeze, so treat it as information rather than a personal verdict. Continue the loop until the offer and the work both make sense for you.
