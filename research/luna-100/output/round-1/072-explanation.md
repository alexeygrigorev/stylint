# Choosing a Portfolio Project from Evidence

A portfolio project is easier to choose when it starts with a direction. Instead of picking a random technology and hoping it becomes relevant, choose a domain, study the companies in it, and identify a problem shared by several teams. This gives you a project you can explain in an interview because you can describe where the idea came from.

The process begins with five to ten companies that hire for the role you want. Current vacancies are useful, but a company can still be relevant when an old opening has closed. Engineering blogs, case studies, and product pages show what teams build and what difficulties they have encountered. The domain is more stable than one particular vacancy, and a project aimed at several companies is less fragile than one designed for a single employer.

Read job descriptions for responsibilities and team context. Read engineering posts for concrete problems. Keep the two kinds of material in separate files while researching so you can return to the evidence. Technology names are clues, but they are not project ideas by themselves. The useful question is what the company uses those technologies for.

Next, extract problems without proposing solutions. For each problem, record who has it, what input they have, what outcome they need, and where the evidence came from. A post about an AI conversation partner, for example, might reveal issues with dialogue quality, grounding, learner feedback, evaluation, or latency. Those are more useful directions than simply copying the listed framework.

Group the problems across companies. A recurring problem is a stronger portfolio target than an isolated one because it gives you several relevant conversations. Once you have a theme, create about five candidates. For every candidate, write down the user, input, output, and reason it fits the domain. This makes the choice comparable before implementation begins.

Choose the stack after choosing the problem. Map technologies to companies or source documents, then select a small set you can actually use. The point is to demonstrate domain-relevant decisions, not to collect every tool mentioned during research.

The project also needs evidence that it works. Add an evaluation harness, logs or monitoring, tests, and a README that a hiring manager can scan. A working end-to-end application is stronger than a technically elaborate repository that cannot process real input. Explain what you built, who uses it, what goes in, what comes out, and how you judge the result.

One project can lead to another. Keep the same domain while varying a model provider, retrieval method, or other implementation choice. Over time, the portfolio shows a coherent interest and a range of skills, and each project has a reason for existing.
