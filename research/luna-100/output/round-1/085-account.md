# The Job Search as a While Loop

The loop does not promise that every application leads to an interview. It gives each attempt a purpose and leaves you with information for the next attempt, which is more useful than waiting for a perfect moment to begin.

I used to describe getting a data science job as an algorithm. The market changed, but the sequence still works for AI Engineering and other engineering roles: network, apply, build, interview, reflect, and repeat. It is a while loop that continues until you receive an offer you actually want.

Networking is not only about finding a referral. Talk to AI engineers and people who work in the field through LinkedIn or meetups. Ask what they build, which technologies they use, whether they are hiring, and how their process works. Junior candidates can also learn from other juniors who recently found a role. These conversations reveal skills to develop and companies you might not have found otherwise.

Apply before you feel completely ready. Job descriptions describe an ideal candidate, and that person often does not exist. If you match some of the requirements, let the hiring team decide. A senior role for a junior candidate or a position where you match only a small fraction of the requirements is a different situation. If relevant roles never respond, review the CV and make its projects and experience match the applications.

Build projects from the skills you see in the market. Timebox them to 10–20 hours when you are exploring. The goal at this stage is awareness of several technologies and an understanding of when to use them. A finished project needs working code in a public repository and a README that explains it clearly. Share the idea, milestones, screenshots or code, and the final link as you work.

Interviews improve through repetition. They may include recruiter, theory, coding, project deep dive, system design, behavioral, or take-home stages, and companies combine them differently. Keep a journal with the questions and tasks. Afterward, write down what was difficult and which answers could have been better. Rejections can result from an internal candidate, a changed role, or a hiring freeze, so they are not always a judgment of your ability.

For an AI Engineering interview, prepare LLM behavior, prompting, context windows, structured outputs, RAG, reranking, agents, evaluation, monitoring, cost, latency, and scalability. A project deep dive may ask what problem you solved, which part you built, how decisions were made, what went wrong, and what you would change.

Take-home assignments deserve judgment too. Clarify the requirements, permitted AI tools, and evaluation criteria. Check that the time estimate matches the work. Before submitting, include tests, an evaluation set, a README, and a demo. If the assignment is unreasonable, existing portfolio projects can sometimes demonstrate the same skills.
