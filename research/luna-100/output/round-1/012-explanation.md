# What I Looked for in a Scholarship Application

The scholarship selection process for the AI Bootcamp began with a particular purpose. It was for people already doing meaningful work who wanted training to increase that work’s impact. It was not a general route into a new career. Previous AI experience was optional, but the application needed to connect a current project with a timely reason for learning.

The first filter was motivation. Short, empty, or generic responses gave little evidence that the applicant had a concrete goal. Applications focused only on finding a job, collecting tools, or making money quickly also missed the scholarship’s intent. A stronger response identified a specific problem, named the people who would benefit, and explained why the Bootcamp was relevant now.

The second question was alignment. An applicant needed to show that they understood what the program taught and how it related to their existing work. A plan to improve a project, reach a community, or multiply an existing effort was more informative than an abstract desire to learn AI. The training was production-focused, so the reason for needing production skills mattered.

The third signal was depth. The application did not need to contain a long technical essay, but it needed enough detail to distinguish a real project from a vague idea. What had the applicant already tried? What problem remained? What would change after the training? Those questions connected the program to work that already existed.

The fourth signal was the likelihood of completion. The Bootcamp was demanding, so the selection process considered whether a candidate could realistically commit time and continue when the material became difficult. Evidence could include a long-term project, a concrete plan, or an accountability structure. This was a prediction about fit and follow-through, not a judgment of someone’s general potential.

Scale changed how the process was implemented. There were more than 2,500 applications, so every submission could not receive the same manual review. Claude Code performed a preliminary pass using custom slash commands that encoded the evaluation logic. It ranked applications and filtered out those that clearly did not match the scholarship. Parallel sub-agents made it possible to process large batches while keeping the instructions consistent.

Automation stopped before the final decision. The top 50 applications were shuffled and reviewed personally. Each was read in full, and linked profiles or projects were checked when available. The AI scores were compared with the substance of the application. Authenticity, clarity of intent, and likely follow-through were more important at this stage than the numeric ranking.

This two-stage process explains why a rejection was not necessarily a verdict on quality. A strong application could be a poor match for the scholarship’s purpose, or competition could exceed the number of places. The useful distinction was between filtering for scale and judging for context. Automation narrowed the field; human review decided whether the story behind an application made sense.
