# What I Learned from Watching People Learn in Public

At DataTalks.Club’s Zoomcamps, we encourage participants to share their progress publicly. They can post updates about assignments and earn bonus points. We noticed that the people who shared tended to stay more engaged, but I wanted to understand what else was happening beyond the course.

Over the years, I interviewed more than 200 practitioners and learners for the DataTalks.Club podcast. Many of them described how public work affected their learning and their careers. The examples were different, but the pattern was consistent enough to organize into a set of benefits. Publishing did not guarantee a job or a business outcome. It made the work visible, gave people a way to respond, and left evidence behind.

Shawn Wang helped popularize the phrase “learning in public.” His point was that staying invisible makes it harder for opportunities to reach you. Pastor Soto gave a concrete example from ML Zoomcamp. He joined as a medical student without an industry role or a LinkedIn presence. After he began sharing his progress, recruiters noticed him without him applying, including a recruiter from Meta. Admond Lee Kin Lim made a related observation: hiring managers remember people who consistently publish relevant work because they can see more than a résumé.

The learning benefit was just as important. Eugene Yan described writing as a way to test understanding. When an explanation is difficult to make clear, the missing understanding usually becomes visible. Pastor experienced that while turning notes into public explanations. He had to understand evaluation metrics instead of skimming them. Publishing also created feedback. Will McGugan shared small updates from his open-source projects and gradually attracted people interested in similar problems. The result was a flow of ideas and corrections without deliberate networking.

The Zoomcamp community made this effect easier to see. Dashel Ruiz Perez said that watching other participants share in Slack made a demanding course feel collective. When someone posted a blocker, others could recognize the same problem and help. Pastor even shared explanations that were incomplete or partly wrong, then used the discussion to correct them. The public mistake became part of the learning process instead of remaining private frustration.

Repeated sharing built something durable as well. Admond connected personal branding with repeatedly helping others. Shawn pointed to posts, talks, and repositories as proof that continues to exist when active networking stops. Pastor noticed that public explanations changed how people saw him, from a student to someone who could apply and explain ideas. For participants, notebooks, repositories, and project write-ups became a portfolio that others could inspect directly.

There were less predictable results. Will described public demonstrations of his open-source work attracting investor attention, eventually leading to a pre-seed funding round. That kind of outcome cannot be planned reliably. It happened because the work was available to be found.

These observations changed how I think about sharing. The point was not to perform expertise or promise that every post would produce an opportunity. It was to make learning easier to explain, easier to correct, and easier for the right people to notice.
