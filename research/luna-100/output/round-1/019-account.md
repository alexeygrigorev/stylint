# Why I Started Researching the AI Engineer Role

Over the previous year, I kept seeing the titles AI Engineer and Gen AI Engineer on job boards, LinkedIn profiles, and community discussions. The names were becoming common, but the role itself did not have a stable definition. What an AI Engineer did depended on the company, the team, and the problem. Expectations could even contradict one another.

The question became especially relevant because I run the AI Engineering Buildcamp. During an Office Hours session, a participant asked for concrete examples of AI Engineer take-home assignments used in interviews. It was a practical question, and I did not have a good reason to answer it from assumptions alone.

I have worked with machine learning and AI for more than 15 years and regularly speak with people whose work fits what many companies call AI Engineering. But I was not employed in that role at the time. I did not want to rely only on indirect experience or conversations, so I began collecting data that I could analyze and share later.

The research started from the market’s two sides. Companies describe expectations in job postings. Candidates show what they can build in projects and take-home assignments. Interviews reveal how those expectations are tested. Looking at all three signals seemed more useful than choosing one official definition for a role that was still changing.

The first source was my own industry experience. I planned to describe how an AI Engineer’s day might be structured, what kinds of problems the role could involve, and which skills might be needed. I wanted to present that perspective first, then compare it with the evidence from the market.

The second source was a dataset of more than 1,500 job postings collected over three months from cities including Los Angeles, New York, London, Amsterdam, and Berlin. Not all postings used the title AI Engineer. Some described AI or generative AI work under other names, and that variation was itself part of the research. The dataset could show how companies named roles, what responsibilities and tools they requested, and where AI Engineering overlapped with ML Engineering.

For interview patterns, I looked at Twitter/X and Reddit discussions and planned conversations with hiring managers, DataTalks.Club community members, and engineers working in these roles. Some conversations might become podcast episodes, while others would remain private but still inform the analysis.

Finally, I collected around 60 public GitHub repositories containing take-home assignment solutions. Research agents helped create better search queries but did not reliably find the requested number of repositories and sometimes hallucinated results. I therefore reviewed and aggregated the repositories manually. The research was becoming a combination of personal experience, structured data, and conversations, because the role itself could only be understood from several imperfect signals.
