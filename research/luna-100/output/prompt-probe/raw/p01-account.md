# Looking for a Definition of the AI Engineer Role

Over the past year, I noticed the titles AI Engineer and Gen AI Engineer appearing more often on job boards, LinkedIn profiles and community discussions. The role itself was still forming, though. There was no stable definition that everyone used, and what an AI Engineer did depended on the company, team and problem.

That uncertainty became practical for me while teaching the AI Engineering Buildcamp. During an Office Hours session, a participant asked for concrete examples of take-home assignments used in interviews. It was a useful question, and I did not want to answer it only from indirect conversations. I have worked with ML and AI for more than 15 years, but I am not currently employed as an AI Engineer. I decided to collect actual data instead.

I wanted to compare three parts of the market: what companies post, what candidates build and how interviews work. The first source was my own experience. I planned to present my view of the role in the first event, then compare it with external evidence.

The second source was a dataset of more than 1,500 job postings collected from the previous three months. The postings came from Los Angeles, New York, London, Amsterdam, Berlin and other cities. Not all used the title AI Engineer. Some described AI or generative AI work under different names, and that naming variation was part of what I wanted to understand. The dataset let me examine responsibilities, repeated skills and the overlap with ML Engineer roles.

I also looked at interview stories shared on Twitter or X and Reddit. Research agents helped collect those discussions. Grok, for example, analyzed X posts and helped identify interview-related conversations. Informal posts can reveal recent interview patterns that official job descriptions leave out.

The fourth source was direct conversations. I planned to speak with people who hire AI Engineers, members of the DataTalksClub Slack community who were interviewing or already doing this work, and engineers I met through LinkedIn. Some conversations could become podcast episodes, while others would stay private and contribute only to the analysis.

Finally, I collected around 60 public GitHub repositories containing take-home assignment solutions. Deep research agents from Google and OpenAI helped me experiment with search, but they did not reliably find 100 repositories and sometimes added hallucinated results. They were more useful for producing better queries. I manually reviewed and aggregated the repositories for local analysis.

The research was still evolving, so I wanted to compare my initial definition with evidence from companies, candidates and interviews. A changing title needs that comparison because one source alone can make a partial role look universal.
