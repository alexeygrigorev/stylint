# Choosing Scholarship Candidates with Context

A scholarship process works better when its purpose is explicit. This one was for people already doing meaningful work who wanted training to increase its impact. It wasn't a general route into a new career. Previous AI experience was optional, but each application needed a connection between current work and a timely reason for learning.

## Start with motivation

Short, empty and generic responses gave little evidence of a concrete goal. Applications focused only on finding a job, collecting tools or making money quickly also missed the scholarship's intent. A stronger response identified a specific problem, named the people who would benefit and explained why the Bootcamp was relevant then.

The next consideration was alignment. Applicants needed to show that they understood what the program taught and how it related to existing work. A plan to improve a project, reach a community or multiply an existing effort was more informative than an abstract desire to learn AI. Because the training was production-focused, the reason for needing production skills mattered.

## Look for depth and follow-through

An application didn't need a long technical essay. It did need enough detail to distinguish a real project from a vague idea. The useful details were what the applicant had already tried, which problem remained and what would change after the training. Those details connected the program to work that already existed.

The Bootcamp was demanding, so I also considered whether a candidate could realistically commit time and continue when the material became difficult. A long-term project, a concrete plan or an accountability structure could provide that evidence. This was a prediction about fit and follow-through, not a judgment of someone's general potential.

## Use automation for scale

More than 2,500 applications made identical manual attention impossible. Claude Code performed a preliminary pass using custom slash commands that encoded the evaluation logic. It ranked applications and filtered out submissions that clearly didn't match the scholarship. Parallel sub-agents processed large batches while keeping the instructions consistent.

Automation stopped before the final decision. I selected the top 50 applications and shuffled them to reduce bias. Then I reviewed each one personally. I read every submission in full and checked linked profiles or projects when applicants provided them.

I compared the AI scores with the substance of the applications. Authenticity, clarity of intent and likely follow-through mattered more than numeric ranking.

## Keep the stages separate

This two-stage process explains why rejection wasn't necessarily a verdict on quality. A strong application could be a poor match for the scholarship's purpose, or competition could exceed the available places. Filtering handled volume. Human review handled context. The distinction let the preliminary system narrow the field without asking it to make the final judgment about a person's situation.
