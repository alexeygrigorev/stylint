# Learning in public

At DataTalks.Club's Zoomcamps, participants can share their learning progress publicly and earn bonus points. We noticed that people who shared tended to stay more engaged. I had also interviewed more than 200 practitioners and learners for the DataTalks.Club podcast, and many described what happened when they made their work visible.

Shawn Wang helped popularize the phrase "learning in public". His point is simple: if your work stays invisible, opportunities are more likely to reach somebody else. Publishing doesn't guarantee an outcome, but it gives recruiters, employers, and collaborators a chance to see what you can do.

Pastor Soto gave a concrete example. He joined ML Zoomcamp as a medical student without an industry role or a LinkedIn presence. During the course he started sharing his progress. Recruiters noticed him without him applying, including a recruiter from Meta. Admond Lee Kin Lim made a similar observation: hiring managers remember people who publish relevant work consistently because they can see evidence beyond a resume.

Writing also tests understanding, and Eugene Yan described public writing as a learning and validation tool. When an explanation won't become clear, the gap is often in the writer's understanding.

Pastor experienced this when he turned notes into explanations. He had to understand evaluation metrics instead of skimming them. Publishing made the missing knowledge visible to him.

Public work brings feedback from people facing similar problems. Will McGugan shared small updates about his open-source projects and gradually attracted people interested in the same technical challenges. The conversations produced ideas and corrections without deliberate networking.

In the Zoomcamp community, Dashel Ruiz Perez said that seeing other participants post in Slack made a demanding course feel collective rather than isolated.

Sharing also creates light accountability. Regular updates show that progress is possible and give a learner a reason to continue. Dashel described this during a demanding course, while Aaisha Muhammad mentioned that leaderboards and visible progress nudged her to finish assignments she might otherwise postpone. When someone shares a blocker, other learners can recognize the same issue and help. Pastor sometimes published incomplete explanations, then used the discussion to correct them.

Over time, the posts become a portfolio. Daniel Egbo encouraged learners to publish notebooks, repositories, and analyses even when the work felt basic. A visible project is easier to evaluate than a statement that someone knows machine learning. Admond connected personal branding with repeatedly helping others, and Shawn described posts, talks, and repositories as durable proof of competence.

The outcomes aren't always planned. Will described public demonstrations of his open-source work attracting investor attention and eventually leading to a pre-seed funding round. That isn't something to optimize for directly. It became possible because the work was available to be found.

Learning in public doesn't guarantee a job, funding, or an invitation. It does make explanations easier to test, mistakes easier to correct, and progress easier for other people to see.
