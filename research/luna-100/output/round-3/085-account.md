# Treating an AI Engineering Search as a Loop

I used to describe getting a data science job as an algorithm. The market changed, but the sequence still works for AI Engineering and other engineering roles.

I use this sequence:

- network
- apply
- build
- interview
- reflect
- repeat

The loop continues until I receive an offer I actually want. It doesn't promise that every application leads to an interview. It gives each attempt a purpose and leaves information for the next one.

Networking isn't only about finding a referral. I talk to AI engineers and other people in the field through LinkedIn or meetups. I ask what they build, which technologies they use, whether they're hiring, and how their process works.

Junior candidates can also learn from juniors who recently found a role. These conversations reveal skills to develop and companies I might not have found otherwise.

I apply before feeling completely ready. Job descriptions describe an ideal candidate, and that person often doesn't exist. If I match some of the requirements, I let the hiring team decide.

A senior role for a junior candidate or a position where I match only a small fraction of the requirements is different. If relevant roles never respond, I review the CV. I make its projects and experience match the applications.

I build projects from skills that appear in the market. While exploring, I timebox each one to 10–20 hours. This lets me learn about several technologies and when to use them before committing to a larger project.

I publish working code in a public repository with a clear README. I share the idea, milestones, screenshots or code, and final link as I work instead of keeping the project invisible.

Interviews improve through repetition.

They can include several stages:

- recruiter interviews
- theoretical interviews
- coding interviews
- project deep dives
- system design
- behavioral interviews
- take-home assignments

Companies combine these stages differently.

I keep an interview journal with questions and tasks. Afterward I write down what was difficult and which answers could be better. An internal candidate, a changed role, or a hiring freeze can also explain a rejection, so it doesn't always measure my ability.

For an AI Engineering interview, I prepare these topics:

- LLM behavior, prompting, and context windows
- structured outputs, RAG, and reranking
- agents, evaluation, and monitoring
- cost, latency, and scalability

A project deep dive may ask what problem I solved and which part I built. It may also ask how decisions were made, what went wrong, and what I would change.

Take-home assignments deserve judgment too. I clarify the requirements, permitted AI tools, and evaluation criteria. I check that the time estimate matches the work.

Before submitting, I include tests and an evaluation set, then add a README and a demo. If the assignment is unreasonable, an existing portfolio project can sometimes demonstrate the same skills.
