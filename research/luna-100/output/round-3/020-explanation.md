# Comparing evidence about the AI Engineer role

Companies don't all mean the same thing when they advertise for an AI Engineer. The work depends on the team and the problems it needs to solve, so expectations can be inconsistent or contradictory. I started researching the role by looking at what employers request and what candidates are asked to build.

My own experience is one source. I've worked with ML and AI for more than 15 years, and I now teach AI Engineering Buildcamp. I have a view of how an AI Engineer's day should work and which skills the role needs.

I'm not currently employed in that position, though, so I want to compare my perspective with actual market data. I'll present my view first, before examining the external evidence.

For job postings, I collected more than 1,500 from the previous three months. They come from cities including Los Angeles and New York, with London, Amsterdam, and Berlin also represented.

I didn't limit collection to jobs titled AI Engineer. Other titles can describe AI or generative AI work, and I want to understand that variation. I can use the postings to compare responsibilities and recurring skill requirements, including tools and overlap with ML Engineer jobs.

Job descriptions don't show everything that happens in an interview. I also looked at stories on Twitter/X and Reddit, where engineers share questions they were asked and describe their work. Research agents helped collect the material, including Grok's analysis of recent X discussions.

I plan to add direct conversations with people who hire AI Engineers. I'll also talk to DataTalks.Club members who are interviewing or working in these roles, plus engineers I meet through LinkedIn.

Some conversations may become public podcast episodes. Others will remain private and still contribute to the research. Together with the social-media discussions, they'll inform a session about interview structure and evaluation criteria, including how hiring decisions are made.

I also collected around 60 public GitHub repositories with take-home assignment solutions. They let me examine what companies actually ask candidates to build outside an interview.

For this collection, I tried deep research agents from Google and OpenAI. They didn't reliably return the 100 repositories I asked for. In some cases, they found one real repository and hallucinated additional ones.

The agents were more useful for improving my search queries. I used them for that step, then manually reviewed the repositories and combined the results for local analysis. I'll use those assignments in the final session of the series, where I can compare what candidates were asked to implement.
