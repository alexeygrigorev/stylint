# A Job Search That Keeps Learning

The job search is easier to manage as a loop than as a single application.

The cycle runs through these activities:

- network to learn the market
- apply to relevant roles
- build projects that show needed skills
- interview and record what happened
- improve and repeat

A conversation can change the project you build, and an interview can reveal a skill to study. The activities stay connected through what each round teaches you.

Networking gives context that job boards don't. Speak with AI engineers, ask about their projects and tools, and learn whether their companies hire.

Junior engineers who recently found jobs can explain which preparation helped and what they would change. A conversation can be useful even when it doesn't produce a referral.

Apply before every requirement feels satisfied. Job descriptions describe an ideal candidate, so don't interpret every bullet as a mandatory gate. There are sensible limits.

A junior candidate should usually avoid a senior role, and matching only a small portion of the requirements is a warning. For a relevant role, let the hiring team evaluate you. If applications produce no response, look at the CV and make its evidence relevant to those roles.

Use market research to choose small projects. Note recurring technologies and timebox each project to 10–20 hours while exploring.

A portfolio project should have public code, a clear README, and a working result. Share the idea, milestones, screenshots, and final link so other people can see how you work. Deeper work can come after you understand which direction fits.

An interview isn't an exam. You're not in school, and the company evaluates you while you evaluate it.

Prepare for several interview formats, starting with recruiter questions about the company and motivation. Recruiters also ask about availability, salary, and your background.

Technical rounds may cover LLMs and RAG. They may then move into agents, evaluation, monitoring, or coding. A project deep dive tests whether the CV matches reality.

System design asks you to clarify requirements, data, and scale before sketching components. Behavioral interviews require examples of collaboration and setbacks.

Set boundaries for take-home tasks by asking how the result is evaluated, whether AI tools are allowed, and what effort is expected. Build tests, an evaluation set, a README, and a demo before submission.

A task that claims two hours but appears to require several days is a warning sign. An automated assignment sent before any conversation is another. It can be reasonable to decline when the time cost is too high and mention comparable public work.

After every interview, keep a journal. Record questions, difficult parts, and better answers for next time. Rejection may reflect a changed role, an internal hire, or a freeze, so treat it as information rather than a personal verdict. Continue the loop until the offer and the work both make sense for you.
