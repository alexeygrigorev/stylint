# Publishing a Local Event Recap While It Still Matters

On August 12, I helped run a one-evening workshop for 34 people. I wanted to publish a recap within 24 hours, while attendees could still use the links and follow up with the speakers.

I wrote this recap as a synthetic style exercise. I invented the event, the 14 photos, the $180 venue fee, and every quotation so I could practice the build-log voice.

In this post, I'll share:

- why I treat a recap as a same-day publication task
- the first capture workflow that failed
- how I organized photos, quotations, and slides
- the editing pass that removed 600 words
- what I published and what still needs work

## The First Attempt

After the last fictional meetup, I waited ten days for a quiet weekend. By then, the presenter had changed jobs, one link in the announcement had expired, and I couldn't remember which question produced the best exchange.

The delay also changed the story. What I wrote felt like a summary of an old calendar entry. It had no urgency for readers and no practical value for people who attended.

I wrote one rule after that draft: publish the recap while the questions and follow-ups are still warm.

## Capture During the Event

For this exercise, I prepared a folder before anyone arrived. It contained one empty text file for timing notes, one for quotations, and one link for uploads. This took about 15 minutes.

I asked one volunteer to photograph the room, the whiteboard, and each speaker twice. She uploaded 14 photos before the closing discussion, and I marked 6 of them as usable. The other 8 were either too dark or contained identifiable faces without permission.

The timing file captured more useful details than the photos:

```text
19:05 arrival and coffee
19:24 demo stopped on permission error
19:36 audience question about test data
20:12 three people asked for the checklist
```

Those timestamps let me reconstruct the evening. The failed demo became a useful part of the story, and the repeated checklist question became the basis for the follow-up resources.

## Collect the Evidence

My first organizational plan was to write everything into one running note. The running note grew to 1,400 words and mixed timing with slides, questions, and reminders.

The draft was accurate and hard to edit. I couldn't tell which sentence belonged in the announcement, the recap, or a private thank-you note.

So I split the note into four plain folders.

The folder names made each artifact easy to find:

```text
photos/
quotes/
slides/
follow-ups/
```

Each quotation went into its own text file with the speaker's name and the minute it occurred. Each follow-up went into a filename that started with the task, such as `share-testing-checklist.txt`. That structure made the next pass mechanical.

## Draft Against a Deadline

I gave the first draft 45 minutes and refused to look for new information during that block. The outline covered the event chronology, the audience discussion, the available resources, and the follow-up steps.

The first version was 1,150 words. It included every talk, every joke, and a detailed description of the coffee setup. It read like a polite diary entry rather than a practical recap.

I used one editing question to cut it. I removed the general introductions and the detailed agenda because an attendee wouldn't click them after leaving the room.

The reduced draft kept three concrete outcomes. It linked to 11 slides, named two tested examples, and included a one-page checklist. It also mentioned the permission failure and the correction, because that detail helped the next speaker.

Quotations were useful evidence, but I checked them carefully. I shortened only filler words, and I sent two quotations to their speakers before publication. One speaker removed a phrase about her employer. The other approved her sentence unchanged.

## Publish the Package

I reduced the final page to 550 words. It included the event summary, two talk highlights, and the audience discussion. I stated the size or duration on every resource link, and a short final paragraph thanked the venue owner.

The photo treatment was simple. I used three pictures, each with a caption naming the speaker, the topic, and the room. An attendee could find the relevant talk from each caption.

I chose photos of the slides and whiteboard rather than close-up shots of people. That choice avoided two potential permission questions.

For distribution, I posted the recap in the event chat and sent it to the 34 registered attendees. I also shared it in one local developer channel. That channel had 210 members, but I expected most clicks to come from the smaller event chat.

In the fictional first day, the page had 86 views and the checklist had 39 downloads. The numbers were smaller than a general newsletter send, but they reached the people who could use the material.

## Remaining Work

The same-day workflow solved the main problem. The published recap preserved the useful examples, and attendees could follow up while the session was fresh.

I still need to add a consent list to the pre-event checklist, because photo permissions came together awkwardly. The follow-up folder also needs an owner and a due date, or it becomes another set of good intentions.

The next event is fictional too, but the process is what I would reuse. I'll write more about event checklists in a future article. Subscribe if you want to follow along.
