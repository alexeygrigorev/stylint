# Running a Cohort Retrospective That Produces Changes

I wrote this synthetic style exercise as a how-to guide. The cohort, dates and numbers are fictional. In June 2026 I finished a six-week data course with 83 students. I wanted a retrospective that ended in shipped fixes rather than a document.

The February edition had a survey, a 31% response rate and zero resulting changes. The June retrospective took one week of evenings and produced five decisions, all shipped within two weeks.

In this post, I'll share:

- how I run the survey while the course is still fresh
- how I pick students for follow-up interviews
- how I group comments into themes
- how themes become decisions with deadlines
- the mistakes to avoid on your first run

## 1. Send The Survey Early

Send the survey in the last week, while deadlines still sting. I use a three-question form that takes four minutes. It asks for the most useful part, the most confusing part and anything the next cohort should drop.

The form has exactly three questions, so students finish it on a phone:

```text
1. What was the most useful part of the course?
2. What was the most confusing part?
3. What should the next cohort drop?
```

Timing made the whole difference. The February survey went out five days after the final session, and 26 of 83 students answered. The June survey went out two days before the final session, and 51 of 83 answered.

Free-text answers hold the real material. Ratings stayed flat between the two cohorts, while the comments named the same complaints over and over. Two comments in June were only one word long, and both still named the same week.

## 2. Interview Five Students

Pick five students who finished and two who dropped out. Finishers tell you what worked, and dropouts tell you where the course lost people.

Each call runs 20 minutes with the same three prompts:

- name the week with the heaviest load
- describe one moment where you almost quit
- pick the one change that would have helped most

I record nothing and take notes in one file during the call, which keeps the pace conversational.

Five interviews take one evening, and the dropouts are the conversations I nearly skipped. One dropout in June explained that homework 4 needed six unpaid hours in a single week, which no survey comment had spelled out.

## 3. Group Comments Into Themes

Paste every comment into one file and mark each line with a short theme. I use a plain text file called `retro-themes.txt`, and tagging the June run took about 90 minutes. The file ended up with 86 lines, one per comment, and 14 theme labels.

Fifty-one survey comments and 35 interview notes became 14 themes. Ten themes had two or fewer mentions, and four themes had more than ten mentions each.

The four heavy themes from June, with mention counts:

```text
homework 4 workload - 23 mentions
late feedback on projects - 17 mentions
week 1 setup instructions - 12 mentions
unclear grading for the final project - 11 mentions
```

Mentions are votes with a margin of error, so I read the raw comments behind every heavy theme. One theme about video length collapsed from 11 mentions to 3 once I removed duplicates from the same study group.

## 4. Turn Themes Into Decisions

A theme without a decision is an apology. Each heavy theme gets one decision with an owner, a deadline and a success check.

The June decisions, written before I closed the laptop for the night:

- split homework 4 into two smaller parts, deadline 10 July
- publish project feedback within five days, starting with the next cohort
- rewrite the week 1 setup guide and test it on a clean laptop, deadline 20 July
- grade the final project against a published rubric, deadline 1 August
- move one optional lecture into a reading list, deadline 15 July

Four of the five shipped by 1 August. The rubric slipped to 9 August, because writing fair grading criteria took two extra evenings.

A retrospective that ends in a document ends in nothing. Every decision gets a date in my task manager, and the next cohort opens with a note about what changed. I wrote the five decisions in the same file as the themes, which kept the evidence one scroll away.

## Common Mistakes

Three mistakes cost me time across the two runs:

- asking for ratings instead of stories, which produced numbers with nothing to fix
- interviewing only finishers, which hid the homework 4 problem until June
- collecting themes without deadlines, which is how the February notes died in a folder

Each mistake has the same fix. Ask for specifics, talk to people who left, and attach a date to every theme.

## After The June Retrospective

The full loop took one week of evenings, from survey link to shipped decisions. The response rate moved from 31% to 61%, and every heavy theme left with a date.

The method has one limit: it depends on me doing the interviews, so a 300-student cohort would need a different plan. I'll keep the survey timing, the five interviews and the dated decisions for the October cohort. The October run gets the same loop with one change: interviews booked before the final session.
