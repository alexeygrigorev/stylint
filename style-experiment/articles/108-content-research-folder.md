# Organizing Research for an Article Before Writing It

Last month I spent nine hours researching an article and then lost 40 minutes trying to find one statistic. I finished the article eventually, but I left the research folder a mess.

I wrote this piece as a synthetic style exercise. I invented the article topic, source count, dates, and research notes so I could practice the build-log voice.

In this post, I'll share:

- the failed research folder I used before
- the four files that now hold an article's evidence
- how I record contradictions between sources
- when I stop collecting and start drafting
- how the folder helps during editing

## Chronological Research Notes

For years, I kept research in a single note named after the article. I pasted links, quotations, numbers, and my own comments in the order I found them.

That approach works while reading, but the note becomes chronological rather than organized by claim when I write.

On the fictional article about course completion rates, the note reached 3,800 words and 27 links. I could remember that a number existed. I couldn't remember whether a platform report, a vendor presentation, or my own calculation produced it.

I rewrote the note twice and still missed one important caveat. That delay convinced me to change the container, rather than try harder.

## Separate Questions and Sources

I now create the new folder before reading.

It contains four files, and each file has one responsibility:

```text
questions.md
sources.md
evidence.md
contradictions.md
```

I keep the reader's problem and my working claims in `questions.md`. Each question gets an ID, such as `Q03`. The one-sentence question names the decision the reader wants to make.

The first three questions looked like this:

```text
Q01: Which completion measures are comparable?
Q02: What course design changes affect completion?
Q03: When is a low completion rate acceptable?
```

I keep records about material in `sources.md`.

Each source record contains the fields I need for later review:

- title and publication date
- access date and link
- source type
- reliability note

I store the extracted evidence elsewhere, so I can delete a source without losing notes.

This small separation made the next steps easier to review.

## Link Evidence to Questions

I use `evidence.md` as the working file.

Each evidence row contains:

- evidence and question IDs
- a quotation or number
- a page or timestamp
- the source ID and my note

One entry from the fictional project looked like this:

```text
E14 | Q02 | "Weekly feedback raised module completion from 41% to 58%"
source S08, page 12, sample 214 learners, instructor-led course
Note: no comparison group
```

The forced linkage exposed a gap immediately. Eleven pieces of evidence belonged to `Q01`, while `Q03` had only two weak entries. I stopped collecting general material and searched for cases where teams accepted low completion.

I also recorded the measurement definition beside every number. "Completion" meant the last watched video in one source and the submitted final project in another. Those numbers can't be compared directly, even when both are expressed as percentages.

I keep quotations short, and I follow the source ID when I need the surrounding context instead of copying entire paragraphs into the evidence file.

## Record Disagreements Early

`contradictions.md` holds cases where credible sources disagree. I used to resolve them silently and choose the convenient number. That habit made the article feel certain and made revision difficult.

Each contradiction now names the competing claims and the conditions behind them:

```text
C02: cohort deadlines improve completion
S08: weekly deadline, instructor contact, completion 58%
S11: self-paced course, no deadline effect, completion 34%
Possible explanation: learner time and course length differ
```

That note became a paragraph in the draft. It says the intervention can help, then states the population and support conditions. The caveat makes the claim less impressive and more usable.

Contradictions also reveal useful article structures. In this fictional case, the disagreement was more informative than the largest percentage increase.

## Stop and Draft

My old stopping rule was to read until the topic felt familiar. That rule rewards anxiety and produces another 20 tabs.

The new rule is practical: stop collecting when every question has evidence and every major claim has at least one condition or counterexample. For this article, that took 19 sources over four sessions.

I then write a claim outline before drafting the explanation. Each line contains a question ID and the evidence IDs that support it. A line without evidence either becomes a personal-experience claim or gets cut.

The first draft took about 2.5 hours, down from roughly 6 hours on a comparable article. The saving came from writing directly beside extracted evidence rather than reconstructing an argument from a chronological note.

I left the remaining open questions in the outline and marked them for a later article.

## Use the Folder While Editing

The folder continues to work after the first draft. During editing, I check each claim against its evidence ID. I can remove a weak source without losing the surrounding argument.

When an editor asks for evidence, I copy the relevant rows into a review note. That takes minutes instead of another research session.

At publication, I keep the folder for 18 months. Platform reports change, and readers often ask why one number differs from a newer study.
