# Keeping a Portfolio of Project Ideas Instead of Starting All of Them

I wrote this synthetic style exercise as an analysis piece. The dates and numbers are fictional. In February 2026 I counted the ideas folder in my notes vault and found 63 one-paragraph project ideas. The same scan showed six abandoned starters in my repos folder, each with a single commit and no README.

The two folders described the same energy spent in opposite directions. Every starter died within three weeks, while the idea notes kept producing useful work. Since then I treat ideas as a portfolio, and I start only a few of them on purpose.

In this post, I'll share:

- why writing an idea down already meets part of the demand
- how a one-paragraph note filters weak ideas
- what reopening old notes says about real demand
- what starting six projects in one quarter cost me
- how the portfolio feeds posts like this one

## The Urge To Start Everything

A new idea arrives with a push toward the editor. In 2025 I obeyed that push six times in one quarter. The results were six folders with a `main.py`, a license file, and one Saturday commit. Each starter felt productive for a weekend and then sat there, taking up space in every repo list I own. None of the six had a user, and two never ran at all beyond the first script.

The counts come from six months of running the portfolio differently, and they're small enough to check by hand. This is self-reported data from my own folders, so treat it as one practitioner's record rather than a study.

## 1. Writing It Down Meets The Demand

The push wants an outlet, and a written note is enough of one. Capturing an idea takes me about three minutes. The note names the trigger, the first user, and the smallest version I could build in one evening.

After the note exists, the push usually dissolves. Of the 63 ideas in the folder, I have started five, and the other 58 stopped itching within a day of being written down. The demand was mostly a demand to remember, and paper memory works.

Ideas arrive at bad times, so the capture path needs zero friction. I dictate most notes into the phone while walking, which is the same brain-dump workflow I use for article openings. The three-minute budget includes the cleanup pass later that evening.

## 2. The Note Doubles As A Filter

Because every note must name a trigger, a first user, and a smallest version, weak ideas fail the template before they fail in code. In March I reviewed all 63 notes against those three fields and marked 41 as weak, usually because no concrete user came to mind. Deleting those 41 lines took ten minutes and prevented, by my estimate, three dead repos.

The filter has a bias, because I write notes on excited evenings and review them on calm ones. Ideas that sound good at both moments are the keepers, and that bar is stricter than my enthusiasm.

One note from January shows the three fields in practice:

```text
idea: retry-backoff tester for webhook apps
trigger: third delivery failure question in one week
first user: me, before the next workshop demo
smallest version: a script that replays recorded failures
```

That idea became one of the five I started, and the note made the first evening fast. Weak notes fail this template in obvious ways, with an empty user line or a smallest version that needs a month.

## 3. Real Demand Comes Back

Time sorts the survivors, so a month after capture I reopen notes related to current work. Seven notes have been reopened three or more times since February. Two of the seven became real projects, and both solved a problem I had hit at least four times that quarter.

The reopen ritual takes ten minutes on Friday, and I sort the folder by reopen count before reading anything. Sorting by count beats sorting by date, because date order buries the persistent ideas under fresh ones.

Reopening has its own bias, because I see my notes weekly and recent work colors what looks urgent. A note that resurfaces in unrelated weeks is the strongest demand evidence I have found.

## 4. Six Starters Cost A Month

The six starters from 2025 consumed about 30 evenings before I admitted they were dead. Each one consumed a weekend of scaffolding and produced zero users, and the folders made every later repo scan slower. The rule I took from it: one evening of capture is cheaper than one weekend of scaffolding.

One starter survived, and it survived because its note had sat open in my editor for five weeks before I wrote any code. The waiting period did the filtering that the weekend usually claims to do.

## The Portfolio In August 2026

The portfolio holds 71 notes as of August 2026. Four are marked in progress, one shipped in June, and the rest wait their turn.

The system still has friction. The pile creates guilt on weeks when I don't review it, and the review ritual drifts whenever a deadline eats the Friday slot. Two notes have waited since February for a decision, and the decision keeps losing to shipped work.

The arc fits one line: capture everything, filter on paper, start only what comes back. Ideas compound when they sit together, and this post started as note 44 in the folder, captured in April and opened four times before today.
