# Making a Code of Conduct Operational

This synthetic style exercise follows a fictional community project with invented details. Every name, count, and timeline below is fictional, and none of it describes real events. Last January the project ran a chat server with 1,900 members and a conduct page nobody enforced.

I administered the server with two moderators, and we settled every conflict by gut feeling. One thread about hiring turned hostile, and we took three days to respond. Two members left before we acted, and both said the rules felt decorative.

In February we wrote a short operating procedure of five pages. It covers reporting, triage, and outcomes with a review schedule. The draft took two evenings plus one uncomfortable call with a former moderator.

In this post, I'll share:

- how reports reach the moderation team
- how triage sorts urgent cases first
- which outcomes we use and when
- how periodic review keeps the document alive
- what changed in the first quarter

## 1. Set Up a Reporting Path Members Trust

Nobody reported problems because nobody knew where reports went. The old conduct page listed values but named no contact and no timeline. I replaced that page with one reporting section in `docs/conduct-ops.md`.

Members can report through a private form or by direct message to any moderator. Both paths reach the same shared inbox, which all three of us read.

The reporting form that feeds the inbox asks for five fields:

```text
reporter contact
date and channel
members involved
what happened, in your own words
links to messages, if available
```

The confirmation tells the reporter what happens next and within what time. We promise first contact within 24 hours, and we have kept that promise in 22 of 23 cases. Reports arrive at a rate of about two per month, which three volunteers can absorb. It's a small promise, and it changed the tone of every later exchange.

## 2. Triage Reports Within Twenty-Four Hours

Every report gets a triage pass on the day it arrives. One moderator reads the report, labels it, and posts a one-line summary in our private channel. The other two confirm the label or argue for a different one.

The triage uses three urgency levels:

- immediate risk to a member, answered the same day
- heated conflict that could spread, answered within 24 hours
- past friction with no ongoing thread, answered within three days

We got the levels wrong at first. Every report sat in the middle level, so nothing felt prioritized. I added example cases to each level, and the labels started matching reality.

One case taught us the main rule. A report sat for a weekend because each of us assumed another had picked it up. The rule I took from that weekend: every report has exactly one named owner by midnight.

Ownership rotates weekly, and the rota lives at the top of the private channel. The owner posts a status line every evening until the case closes. That evening line takes two minutes, and it ended the silent-assumption problem.

## 3. Choose Outcomes From a Fixed List

Gut-feeling punishments felt arbitrary, so we fixed five possible outcomes. The list lives in the same document, right after the urgency levels. Members can read the full list, so nobody meets a surprise outcome.

The five outcomes run from light to heavy:

- private note explaining which rule applied
- public reminder in the thread, without naming anyone
- temporary mute for three days
- removal from the server with a written reason
- permanent ban, decided by all three moderators together

We used the private note 14 times in the first quarter, out of 23 total reports. Temporary mutes happened four times, and removals happened twice. There's a line here - the line between a heated debate and targeted behavior, and the examples mark it.

The written reason matters more than the outcome. Every outcome includes two sentences: which behavior broke which rule, and what happens on repeat. I can't defend decisions from memory, so every decision gets its two sentences on the day.

## 4. Review the Document Every Quarter

A conduct document rots when nobody rereads it. Ours gets a 45-minute review call in the first week of each quarter. All three moderators attend, and we read the full five pages aloud.

The review answers three fixed prompts:

- which reports felt hard to classify
- which examples need updating
- which timelines did we miss and why

In the January review we updated the urgency examples and shortened the mute from seven days to three. Shorter mutes get used, while week-long mutes felt too heavy to apply. It stays useful because we reread it every quarter.

## Results After Three Months

Reports rose from 4 in the previous quarter to 23, and I read that rise as trust rather than decay. Two reporters told us the confirmation message convinced them to write. Members report early friction now instead of leaving quietly. Response time dropped from three days to a median of nine hours.

The work taught me a narrower lesson than write better rules. A conduct page works when members can see the path from report to outcome. The procedure is five pages, and the team is three volunteers with day jobs.
