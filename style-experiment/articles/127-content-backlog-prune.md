# Pruning a Content Backlog That Became a Junk Drawer

I wrote this synthetic style exercise as a build log. The projects, dates and numbers below are fictional.

On 14 July 2026, I opened my writing backlog and found 214 entries. The oldest idea had waited for 31 months. Several lines said only "automation", "course idea" or "agent thing", with no explanation of the problem they solved.

The backlog had turned into a junk drawer. Every capture felt safe because it avoided an immediate decision. Reading the whole file had become a project, so I postponed it again.

In this post, I'll share:

- how I grouped 214 ideas in one afternoon
- the four fields I used to score them
- which 138 entries I deleted
- how I handled ideas that were interesting but vague
- what the smaller backlog looks like now

## Too Many Unmade Decisions

I had three capture places: an Apple Notes folder, a Markdown file and 46 unread newsletter drafts. During a trip in March I copied them into one file. I believed that consolidation would solve the problem.

It solved discovery and made avoidance easier. The file had a title for every idea and a decision for almost none. Some entries were duplicate angles on the same topic. Some referred to tools I no longer use. Nine had already become articles, but nobody had removed them.

My first attempt used tags. I created tags for audience, effort, topic and urgency, then tagged 23 entries. That took almost 90 minutes and produced no next action. The file still had 191 untagged entries, so I stopped.

The rule I took from that attempt was simple: pruning needs a decision rule before it needs a taxonomy.

## A Simple Scoring Rule

I set a timer for 20 minutes and scored only titles that already made sense.

Each idea received four ratings:

- last touched, in months
- demand, based on questions or searches
- effort, as small, medium or large
- unique point, as absent, weak or clear

I used a spreadsheet because sorting is easier there than in Markdown.

Demand was deliberately coarse:

- a score of 2 meant people had asked about the topic more than once
- a score of 0 meant I only liked the sound of the topic

The unique-point field did the most work. "Build a dashboard" scored zero because it made no claim. "Why a personal dashboard needs a weekly roll-up before charts" scored two because it made a claim I could test with fictional publishing data.

After two hours, I had scored 171 of the original 214 entries. I handled the remaining 43 with a separate decision.

## The First Prune

I deleted entries that met two conditions: untouched for 18 months or more, and no clear unique point. There were 92. I also deleted 27 duplicates and 19 completed ideas. That brought the file to 76 lines.

Some deletions were uncomfortable, especially the retrieval-evaluation stub. It had a clear point and 11 months of age. I had started it twice, and both starts were buried in old branches. I deleted the article stubs but kept one 40-word paragraph in a lessons file.

Another entry was "write about my home server". It had demand from two friends and no specific claim. I changed it to "How I keep three home services observable with one dashboard". That made the effort visible and moved it from someday to this quarter.

The target wasn't a fixed count. I stopped deleting when every surviving line answered the same reader question about the action to take after finishing the article.

## Ambiguous But Interesting

The 43 unscored entries fell into three groups:

- vague phrases that had lost their original context
- project ideas that belonged in an engineering backlog
- course ideas that depended on a future cohort

I moved 11 project ideas into a separate repository. They now sit beside infrastructure tickets rather than pretending to be article prompts. Eight course ideas went into one file called "course seeds", with a note to reconsider them after the fictional autumn cohort.

That left 24 vague phrases, and I deleted 19 without ceremony. The remaining five were one-line reminders such as "CLI ergonomics" and "feedback triage". I expanded each into a concrete task and gave it a maximum age. If an idea stays unscored for two more months, I'll delete it.

This step matters because a backlog should be reviewable. Keeping an ambiguous phrase out of guilt hides the decisions that actually need attention.

## The Smaller Backlog

The new backlog has 76 entries in three files. Twenty-two are ready, which means they have a claim, an example and a first source. Thirty-one need evidence. Twenty-three need scope. No line is older than 31 months without one of those labels.

Every Monday, I review the ready list for 15 minutes. I choose one idea to develop and one to delete. The scoring spreadsheet stays closed during that review. Its job was the initial cut, and maintaining 171 ratings would recreate the original burden.

I also changed capture so a new idea must include a proposed reader action. Otherwise it goes into a separate inbox, which has 14 items now. That visible number can't grow quietly for another two years.

The system is boring by design. It has no automatic priority, no publishing calendar and no claim that every idea deserves an article.

## Closing Notes

The useful output was 138 deletions and 22 actionable drafts, rather than a perfect ranking. The four fields were enough to force choices. The separate inboxes made the difference stick.

A backlog is a decision queue. When it contains only captured intentions, it protects old ambiguity at the expense of current work. I plan to repeat this review after the next 50 entries arrive.
